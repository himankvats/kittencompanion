# Scooter Wouldn't Eat

*A first-person diagnostic on the first four months of new cat ownership, and the gap between what owners know they need and what's actually available to them when they need it.*

---

The kitten we brought home was named Scooter. He was small enough to fit in one hand. We had done what we thought was the responsible amount of preparation — food bowl, litter box, a small bag of the kibble the shelter had been feeding, a carrier, a cat tree we'd assembled the night before. We had read articles. We had a vet appointment scheduled for the following week.

On day two, Scooter stopped eating.

He had eaten a little on day one — picking at the kibble we'd set out, drinking some water, exploring under the couch and behind the refrigerator the way kittens do. By the morning of day two, his bowl was full of yesterday's food, untouched. By the afternoon I was on Google.

The internet told us first that the food was wrong. Cats are picky. Some kittens refuse certain textures. We drove to the pet store and bought ten different kinds — pâté, shreds, gravy, kitten-specific formulas, a small can of the high-end stuff that costs more per ounce than my own groceries. Back home, we lined them up on the kitchen floor in tiny saucers like a tasting menu for a creature who weighed less than a paperback. Scooter sniffed two and walked away.

Next the internet told us he wasn't comfortable in the new environment. He needed time. He needed a small, safe space. We made one. We added a worn t-shirt that smelled like me, because some article said this would help. We dimmed the lights. We tried not to handle him.

He still wasn't eating.

By the next morning we were scared. A kitten that doesn't eat is on a much shorter clock than an adult cat — hepatic lipidosis can develop in days if calorie intake drops. We called the vet. They fit us in that afternoon, examined him, ran the usual checks, found nothing acutely wrong. They sent us home with some appetite-stimulating advice and told us to come back if it continued.

Walking out of the office I started talking to another woman in the waiting room who was there with her own kitten. She asked how ours was settling in. I told her. She asked, almost as an aside, whether he had come from a litter where he was particularly attached to a sibling.

He had. The shelter had mentioned it. We hadn't thought it mattered.

She told us that some kittens — especially ones taken early from a sibling they were bonded to — go through what's essentially a mourning period when they're rehomed alone. They under-eat for a few days. They withdraw. Most of them come out of it on their own, but the trick is to be physically present with them, to be the new attachment, rather than to leave them alone in a "safe space" while they readjust.

We went home and sat on the floor with Scooter for two hours. We pet him. We talked to him. We didn't try to feed him. Around the second hour he climbed into my partner's lap and started purring. Later that evening he ate a small amount of one of the foods we'd already tried.

He was, from that point on, fine.

## What just happened, mechanically

The information that solved our problem was: *some kittens experience a transient feeding refusal in the first few days after rehoming due to sibling separation; the intervention is physical presence with the new owner, not isolation in a quiet space.*

That information exists. It's in cat behavior literature. It's discussed in shelter staff training. Many experienced cat owners know it. It was not, however, in any of the first two pages of Google results we read at 11pm on day two. It was not raised by the vet during our urgent visit — partly because the vet's job is to rule out clinical causes first, and partly because by the time we got there, the situation looked acute enough that they weren't going to bet our kitten's life on a behavioral hypothesis.

The information was eventually conveyed to us, accurately, by a stranger in a waiting room.

That conveyance was correct, useful, timely, and entirely coincidental. If she hadn't been there, or if we hadn't talked to her, we would have continued down the food-rejection track for another day or two before either lucking into the right answer or finding ourselves in a much worse situation.

This is the failure mode this project is about. The information new cat owners need is mostly known. It is not routed to them at the moments they need it, in a form they can use.

## The second pattern, weeks later

The other thing we did wrong, we did slowly.

Scooter, by the time he was a few weeks old and clearly thriving, was extremely cute. He would chase a ball under the couch and look proud of himself. He would jump for a toy and execute a small mid-air twist. He would walk into a room, sit down, and look at us with the kind of expression humans evolved over hundreds of thousands of years to be unable to ignore.

We gave him treats. We gave him a lot of treats. Look at him, he's been so good. He played for two whole minutes with the wand toy, let's give him a treat. He's purring in his sleep, let's give him a treat when he wakes up. The treats were small, none of them were individually a problem, and we weren't worried about the cost.

By the time Scooter was five months old he had developed a very clear theory of treats. Treats were how the humans communicated. Treats arrived frequently. The appropriate response to wanting one was to demand one. He sat on the kitchen counter where he was not supposed to be and meowed. He stood in the doorway at 6am and meowed. He sat in front of the treat cabinet and meowed in a way that seemed engineered to be the most psychologically destructive frequency a small mammal can produce.

Resetting this took weeks.

The mechanics were simple: stop reinforcing the demanding behavior, establish a schedule for treats, hold the schedule. The hard part was the ignoring. Cats can sit and meow for a very long time. They can meow when you're trying to sleep, when you're on a work call, when you're mid-conversation with someone visiting your apartment. The behavior gets *louder* before it gets quieter — the technical term is an extinction burst — and at the loud part of the extinction burst you will feel like you're being cruel to an animal who is so clearly asking for something, even if what they're asking for is more of what caused the problem.

We got through it. Scooter today is a cat with a schedule. He knows when food happens and when treats happen, and mostly does not demand them outside those windows. But the cost was real. We had spent weeks teaching him a behavior, and weeks unteaching it, and we created a period of stress for everyone involved that was entirely preventable.

## What just happened, mechanically (again)

The information that would have prevented this was: *intermittent reinforcement of any behavior, especially one a cat will repeat to seek attention or food, builds habit much faster than most owners realize; treat schedules should be deliberate from the start, not intuitive.*

This information also exists. It's in cat behavior literature and in any decent cat-training book. But it isn't the kind of information a new owner thinks to look up, because in the moment, the behavior you're reinforcing doesn't *look* like a problem. It looks like a cute kitten getting a treat. Every individual instance is fine. The accumulated pattern is the part that isn't fine, and the accumulated pattern is invisible until weeks later, when you can no longer eat dinner without negotiating.

So there are two different failure mechanisms. The kitten-not-eating story is an *acute event* with a single resolution — the right information at the right time would have solved it in hours instead of days. The treats story is a *gradual accumulation* with no single moment to fix — there is no day-two equivalent, just a slow drift that becomes visible only in retrospect.

A new cat owner is exposed to both kinds of failure throughout the first four months. The information needed to handle either is mostly available, mostly free, mostly already published somewhere. It is also, in practice, almost impossible to find at the moment you need it, because the moment you need it is the moment you didn't know to look.

## What the vet sees

The vet visit, in our case, was helpful for what it was. It ruled out clinical causes, which mattered. But the vet was working from very limited information. We told them Scooter had stopped eating. We told them how long. We told them we'd tried different foods. They examined him for the things vets examine for in that situation.

What the vet did not have, and could not have had, was any longitudinal picture of Scooter's day. They did not know that he had been eating a few bites per hour during the first day and had then stopped, rather than refusing food from the start. They did not know whether he was still drinking water normally. They did not know how much he was sleeping versus exploring. They did not know whether he was using the litter box. They asked us most of these questions, and we answered to the best of our recollection — which, being honest, wasn't very good. We had been panicking for thirty hours and our memory of his behavior was already smeared.

The vet appointment is built around the assumption that the owner is a reliable historian. New cat owners, in the most stressful moments, are almost never reliable historians, because the experience itself is overloading.

This is a gap. The vet doesn't need a diary of every observation. The vet needs, ideally, a *thirty-second read* on what the cat's normal looks like and where the deviation started. Did appetite drop suddenly or gradually? Was activity level changing before the eating did? Have litter habits been consistent? What did a normal day look like a week ago, and what does it look like now?

That information exists in the household. The owner is observing all of it. It just isn't being captured in a structured way, so it can't be referenced when it matters.

## The pattern, generalized

Two failure modes — the acute event and the slow accumulation — define the first four months of new cat ownership. Both have similar properties: the information needed to handle them exists, it isn't routed to owners at the right time, the owners themselves are too overloaded to seek it efficiently, and the eventual cost is paid in stress, money, and occasionally in a cat's wellbeing.

The vet is positioned to help but only intermittently, only briefly, and only with whatever the owner can remember to convey during a short appointment.

There is no equivalent of the eye doctor's follow-up visit here. Vet checkups happen at vaccination intervals, not at owner-overwhelm intervals. The owner is alone with the kitten between visits, and the kitten is, by design, a small animal that cannot tell anyone what is wrong.

The four-month window is where most of the formative experience happens. The cat is establishing baseline behaviors — eating patterns, litter habits, sleep rhythm, response to humans, response to other animals if there are any in the household. The owner is establishing baseline confidence — whether they trust their own judgment, whether they know when to escalate to a vet, whether they're building habits that will compound positively or negatively.

What gets built in that window mostly stays built. A confident owner with a well-adjusted cat at month four tends to remain a confident owner with a well-adjusted cat at year four. An overwhelmed owner with a cat full of accidentally-reinforced quirks at month four tends to compound the problems they already have.

So the four months matter. They are mostly unsupervised. The information that would help is mostly published somewhere and mostly inaccessible at the moment of need. That is the diagnostic. The rest of this project's documents pick up from there.

---

*A note on the writing: this is a real story about a real kitten. Scooter exists. The pet store trip happened. The waiting-room conversation happened. The treats-induced meowing happened. The reason this case study works as a diagnostic and not just an anecdote is that everything that happened to us is happening to many other new cat owners right now, in slightly different forms, for the same underlying reasons. The patterns are general. The cat is specific.*

*And yes, by month six we were absolutely the kind of people who would have referred to ourselves as Scooter's pet parents. We're aware that this is a phrase some people find irritating. We're unbothered.*
